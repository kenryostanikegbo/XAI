## Recommendations for Future Research

Six directions for future research follow directly from the limitations identified in §5.3 and from the open questions raised in §4.5–§4.8.

### Direction 1 — Temporal Prediction at Multiple Points Within the Presentation

The present study predicts the final outcome from features aggregated over the full presentation (Chapter 3 §3.5). A natural extension is to predict the final outcome from features aggregated up to each week of the presentation, producing a sequence of weekly models. This extension would address three open questions. First, how early in the presentation can the model identify failing students? The answer is institutionally important: an early-warning system that flags a student in week 2 of a 30-week presentation is more useful than one that flags them in week 28. Second, does the feature ranking change across weeks? The present study aggregates over the full presentation and cannot answer this; a temporal extension could show whether early signals are dominated by demographic features (which are fixed) and late signals by engagement features (which accumulate). Third, do the borderline cases shift across weeks? The borderline cases in §4.7 are defined relative to the final prediction, and a temporal analysis could show whether students who are borderline at the end are also borderline at week 10 or whether they cross the decision boundary mid-presentation.

### Direction 2 — Threshold-Sensitivity Analysis for Operating-Point Selection

The present study used a default 0.5 probability threshold for both classifiers, and the F1 gap of 0.020 between the Random Forest and the Logistic Regression depends on that choice (Chapter 4 §4.5). A threshold-sensitivity analysis in which the operating point was varied between 0.3 and 0.7 would clarify whether the gap is stable across operating points or whether it is an artefact of the default. The analysis should also report the precision, recall, and F1 at each threshold, and should identify the threshold that maximises a deployment-relevant utility function (for example, the expected number of correctly flagged failing students minus the expected cost of an unwarranted outreach contact). The present study did not perform this analysis because the deployment context is institution-specific and the utility function was not specified, but the analysis is straightforward to add to notebook 05.

### Direction 3 — Cross-Method Robustness Check on Standardised Features

The cross-model comparison in §4.8 is partly confounded by the fact that the two classifiers see the features on different scales (Chapter 5 §5.3 limitation 5). A clean robustness check would train a second Random Forest on the standardised features and recompute the Spearman correlation and the top-10 Jaccard index. If the two Random Forests (original-scale and standardised) produce similar rankings, then the scale effect is small and the divergence reported in §4.8 is dominated by model family. If they produce different rankings, then the scale effect is large and the divergence reported in §4.8 should be reinterpreted. The robustness check is conceptually simple and could be added to notebook 04 as a third model variant, with the corresponding SHAP and comparison logic in notebooks 06 and 08.

### Direction 4 — Cross-Institutional Comparison

The present study used one dataset from one institution, and the generalisability of the findings to other institutions, other modes of study, and other national contexts is an open question (Chapter 5 §5.3 limitation 1). A natural extension is to apply the same methodology to two or three comparable datasets (for example, a US MOOC dataset, a European university learning-records-store dump, or a private-sector corporate-learning dataset) and compare the rankings. If the engagement-led ranking holds across datasets, then the engagement hypothesis is supported as a general claim about online learning. If it fails to replicate, then the engagement hypothesis is an artefact of the OULAD context and the present findings would not generalise. Either result would be informative, and the cost of the replication is modest because the methodology is already implemented.

### Direction 5 — Causal Interpretation of Feature Contributions

The SHAP values reported in Chapter 4 are statistical associations, not causal effects (Chapter 2 §2.4). A natural extension is to combine the predictive model with a causal-inference framework (for example, a structural causal model or a double-machine-learning estimator) to identify which feature contributions represent causal levers and which represent mere correlations. This extension is methodologically demanding: it requires either a randomised experiment or a strong assumption of conditional unconfoundedness, and the OULAD dataset does not contain a randomised intervention by design. But the institutional payoff is large: a tutor who knows that *increasing* a student's engagement will *reduce* their failure risk can act on that knowledge; a tutor who only knows that engagement is *associated* with failure risk cannot. The present study does not support a causal reading, and any future study that aimed to support one would need to address the unconfoundedness assumption explicitly.

### Direction 6 — Beyond Binary Classification: Predicting Withdrawal and Predicting Marks

The present study excludes Withdrawn students and treats Pass and Distinction as a single class (Chapter 3 §3.4). A natural extension is a three-class problem that distinguishes Pass, Fail, and Withdrawn, with the corresponding feature rankings reported for each class. A more ambitious extension is a regression problem that predicts the student's final mark on a continuous scale rather than a binary label. Both extensions would require additional feature engineering (for example, the date of withdrawal as a target value, or the final mark as a continuous outcome) and a different set of evaluation metrics, but the methodology is conceptually similar. The institutional payoff is a more nuanced view of student risk: a student at risk of withdrawal is in a different category from a student at risk of failing, and a student at risk of a low mark is in a different category from a student at risk of failing outright.
