## Synthesis and Discussion of Findings

This section synthesises the analytical findings of §4.5–§4.8 against the four research questions set in Chapter 1 §1.5 and the methodological commitments of Chapter 3. The aim is integrative rather than additive: the four preceding sections each address one research question in isolation, whereas this section reads them together to expose the higher-order pattern — namely, that predictive accuracy and explanatory coherence pull in slightly different directions, and that the choice of model family is therefore not a free parameter.

### Cross-Cutting Theme: Accuracy versus Explanatory Coherence

The Random Forest leads Logistic Regression on five of six predictive metrics (accuracy, recall, F1, ROC-AUC, AP; §4.5), but the lead is modest — F1 gap of 0.020, ROC-AUC gap of 0.005 — and is concentrated on recall, the metric most sensitive to threshold choice. The two classifiers are statistically indistinguishable on AUC at conventional thresholds, and the F1 lead is threshold-dependent. On the explanatory side, the two models diverge more sharply: Spearman ρ = 0.10 over the 44-feature importance vectors (§4.8), top-10 Jaccard = 0.25, and only four features appear in both top-10 lists. The aggregate pattern is therefore *agreement on outcome, divergence on reason*: the two models flag mostly the same students as failing, but they point to different features when justifying that flag. This is not a contradiction; it is the expected behaviour of a linear and a tree-ensemble model facing a problem where behavioural and structural signals both carry information.

### Joint Reading of the Four Research Questions

Research Question 1 (predictive performance) is settled: the Random Forest is the marginally stronger classifier, and the gap rests on its higher recall on the minority class (§4.5). Research Question 2 (global feature importance) shows a clear two-track structure: Logistic Regression leans on demographic and course-context features, Random Forest leans on engagement and assessment features, and only `submission_rate`, `weighted_mean_score`, `edu_A Level or Equivalent`, and `gender_F` survive in both top-10 lists (§4.6, §4.8). Research Question 3 (cross-model agreement) quantifies that divergence: ρ = 0.10, Jaccard = 0.25, and a structural-versus-behavioural split in the LR-only and RF-only features (§4.8). Research Question 4 (local interpretability) shows that the two models agree on direction for the four corners of the confusion matrix but disagree on the two borderline cases near the decision boundary (§4.7, §4.8). Read jointly, these four answers describe a coherent story: the engagement-and-assessment hypothesis is supported by both models on the global level, the demographic-and-context hypothesis is supported primarily by Logistic Regression, and the two hypotheses are complementary rather than competing.

### Why the Divergence Is Real, Not an Artefact

The cross-model divergence is partly a function of model family and partly a function of feature-engineering choices. On the model-family side, Logistic Regression can only express additive effects, so it assigns weight to whichever features carry the strongest marginal signal — in this dataset, demographic and course-context features that vary systematically with the Fail class. Random Forest can express non-linear interactions, so it routes weight to whichever features combine usefully with others — in this dataset, engagement metrics whose predictive value depends on the level of other engagement metrics (the interaction effect visible in the wider RF beeswarm, §4.6). On the feature-engineering side, the two models see the same 44 features but on different scales (standardised for LR, original-scale for RF), and TreeSHAP and LinearSHAP are not directly comparable in magnitude (§4.6, §4.8). The author cannot, on the basis of these results alone, separate model-family effects from scale effects. A robustness check in which the Random Forest is also evaluated on the standardised features would clarify the scale component, and that check is left for future work.

### Operational Implications

For institutional deployment, the choice between the two models depends on the question the institution is asking. If the question is *which students are at risk?*, the Random Forest gives a slightly more complete answer (recall 0.831 versus 0.789, §4.5). If the question is *why are they at risk?*, the two models give different answers — the Logistic Regression explanation points to demographic and structural factors, the Random Forest explanation points to engagement and assessment factors (§4.6, §4.7). Neither explanation is wrong; they are descriptions of the same students through different feature lenses. A useful early-warning system would not commit to a single model family but would present both explanations side by side, letting a tutor triangulate between the two feature lenses. The borderline cases (§4.7, §4.8) are exactly the students where the two lenses disagree most strongly, and these are the students most in need of human judgement.

### Methodological Limitations Visible in the Results

Two limitations of the present analysis are visible directly in the results. First, both models were tuned on F1 with a default 0.5 threshold; the apparent F1 lead of the Random Forest depends on that threshold (§4.5). A threshold-sensitivity analysis would be needed to claim a stable performance gap, and that analysis is left for future work. Second, the cross-model comparison is computed on the 300-row stratified SHAP subsample for the Random Forest (§4.6), so the Random Forest global ranking reflects that subsample; the Logistic Regression global ranking is computed over the full 6,732-row test set. The two rankings are therefore not strictly comparable in sample size, although the stratified subsample preserves the 31.4 per cent Fail prevalence of the full test set. Both limitations are minor and do not invalidate the qualitative findings, but they should be flagged when the results are read.

### Closing Note on Chapter 4

Chapter 4 has answered four research questions with one shared finding: behavioural engagement and demographic context both matter, but they matter differently for different model families. The next chapter takes the institutional, methodological, and forward-looking implications of this finding and turns them into conclusions and recommendations.
