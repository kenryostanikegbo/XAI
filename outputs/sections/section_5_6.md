## Concluding Remarks

This study set out to investigate whether explainable machine learning could predict student failure on the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset, and whether the explanations the models produced would be operationally useful. The investigation answered four research questions, met six research objectives, and produced a result that is both reassuring and uncomfortable.

The reassuring part is that the methodology works. Both classifiers achieve strong predictive performance on a held-out test set (ROC-AUC above 0.95, F1 above 0.84), and the SHAP-based explanations are stable enough to be inspected at the per-instance level. The pipeline is reproducible from a clean clone in under 45 minutes, and every column of the modelling matrix can be reconstructed from the raw OULAD CSVs alone. The methodology is therefore mature: a researcher who wanted to apply the same approach to a comparable dataset could do so with modest additional effort.

The uncomfortable part is that the two classifiers do not agree on the reasons. The Spearman rank correlation between the two mean-|SHAP| vectors is 0.10, the top-10 Jaccard index is 0.25, and the two models point to different feature families when justifying the same predictions. This is not a failure of either model — it is a property of the problem. Student failure is a multi-determined outcome, and behavioural engagement and demographic context both carry information about it. A linear model and a tree-ensemble model extract that information through different mechanisms, and the result is two different explanations of the same students.

The institutional implication is that the choice of model family is not a free parameter. An institution that deploys only one of the two classifiers will see only one of the two explanations, and will therefore systematically bias its view of its students. The cleanest deployment is a two-model deployment: both classifiers run, both explanations are presented, and the tutor triangulates. This is operationally modest — it adds one model to the pipeline — but it removes a known source of systematic bias. The borderline cases, where the two explanations disagree most, are the cases most in need of human judgement, and the institutional response should be most intensive for those cases.

The methodological implication is that the present study cannot, on the basis of observational data alone, separate model-family effects from scale effects or feature-engineering effects. The two classifiers see the features on different scales, the SHAP values are not directly comparable in magnitude, and the divergence reported in Chapter 4 §4.8 is therefore partly a function of the pipeline rather than purely a function of the models. A future study that trained both classifiers on the same standardised features would clarify this, but the present study does not, and the conclusions are bounded accordingly.

The forward-looking implication is that the most useful next step is not a more accurate model but a more useful deployment. The present study has shown that the methodology can predict student failure at a level of accuracy that would be operationally useful, and that the explanations can be inspected at the per-instance level. What remains is to deploy the methodology in an institutional setting, validate it on a subsequent cohort, and refine it on the basis of tutor feedback. The present study cannot perform that validation, but it can point the way.

The final word is this: explainable machine learning is not a replacement for tutor judgement, but it is a useful complement to it. The present study has shown that the two classifiers can be trained, explained, and compared on a real-world educational dataset. The institutional challenge now is to use the explanations well: to read them carefully, to act on them appropriately, and to remember that a statistical association is not a causal lever. Done well, an early-warning system built on this methodology would catch more failing students and would give tutors a clearer view of why those students are at risk. Done poorly, it would automate bias and would mislead the people it is meant to support. The choice between those outcomes is not a methodological choice — it is an institutional one.
