## Recommendations for Institutional Practice

Five recommendations follow from the present findings for any institution considering the deployment of an explainable machine-learning system to predict student failure.

### Recommendation 1 — Present Both Explanations Side by Side

The two classifiers identify different drivers of student failure, and the difference is not noise (§4.8). An institution that deploys only one of the two classifiers will present only one of the two explanations to its tutors, and will therefore systematically bias the tutor's view of the student. The cleanest institutional deployment is therefore a two-model deployment: train both classifiers, present both predictions, and present both SHAP explanations. Tutors can then triangulate between the two explanations, and the borderline cases — where the two explanations disagree most — are exactly the cases most in need of human judgement (§4.7). This recommendation is operationally modest: it adds one model to the deployment pipeline, and it adds one extra panel to the tutor-facing dashboard, but it removes a known source of systematic bias from the institutional decision.

### Recommendation 2 — Use the Random Forest for Catchment and the Logistic Regression for Justification

The Random Forest has the higher recall on the minority class (0.831 versus 0.789, §4.5), so it catches more failing students and misses fewer. The Logistic Regression explanation is easier to communicate to a non-technical audience, because every feature contributes a single signed weight and there are no interactions to explain (§4.6). A natural hybrid deployment is therefore to use the Random Forest to *flag* students and the Logistic Regression to *justify* the flag. The two outputs would be presented together: a tutor receives a list of flagged students (from the Random Forest) with a short justification (from the Logistic Regression). The justification would not match the flag exactly in every case, but that is itself a useful piece of information — it tells the tutor that the two models agree on the outcome but disagree on the reason, and that the student is a candidate for closer inspection. The borderline cases (§4.7) are the ones where the disagreement is largest, and they are the ones where the institutional response should be most intensive.

### Recommendation 3 — Treat the Explanations as Decision Support, Not Decision Automation

The SHAP explanations describe which features pushed the prediction toward Fail and which pushed it toward Pass (§4.7). They do not describe what the tutor should do. A feature contribution is a statistical association, not a causal claim, and the institutional response to a flagged student should be informed by the explanation but not determined by it (Chapter 2 §2.4). The present study cannot, on the basis of observational data alone, support a causal reading of any feature contribution, and any deployment that treated SHAP values as causal would be misusing the tool. The conservative institutional practice is to use the SHAP explanations as a triage aid: the tutor sees the explanation, decides whether the explanation makes sense given what they know about the student, and then decides on a response based on their judgement. The model does not decide; it informs.

### Recommendation 4 — Validate on a Subsequent Cohort Before Scaling Up

Both models were trained and evaluated on the same OULAD cohort, with a 70/30 split and `random_state=42` (Chapter 3 §3.6). The cross-validated performance estimates reported in §4.5 are honest estimates of within-cohort performance, but they are not estimates of cross-cohort performance. An institution that intends to deploy the model on a subsequent cohort — for example, the next presentation year — should validate the model on that cohort before scaling up the deployment. The validation should include not only the headline metrics (precision, recall, F1) but also a spot-check of the explanations on a handful of students, to confirm that the model is pointing to features the institution believes are relevant. A model whose explanations look sensible in cross-validation but look nonsensical on the new cohort is a model whose deployment should be paused.

### Recommendation 5 — Invest in Tutor Training on SHAP Outputs

The SHAP waterfall plots in §4.7 are not self-explanatory. A tutor who has never seen a SHAP plot before cannot interpret one without training, and a tutor who misinterprets one may make worse decisions than a tutor who has no plot at all (Chapter 2 §2.4). The institutional cost of a SHAP dashboard therefore includes a training cost, and that cost is not negligible. The training should cover three things: what a SHAP value means (a feature contribution to a single prediction, not a causal effect), how to read the waterfall plot (the features are sorted by signed magnitude, the colour encodes feature value, and the base value is the average prediction), and how to act on borderline cases (where the SHAP sum is small in absolute terms, the prediction is unstable, and the tutor's judgement should override the model). Without this training, the dashboard is a liability rather than an asset.
