## Limitations of the Study

Five limitations bound the conclusions that can be drawn from this study, and each is worth stating explicitly.

### Limitation 1 — Single Dataset, Single Institution

The Open University is one institution, and the OULAD is one dataset. The Open University is unusual in three respects: it is a distance-learning institution, its student body is older and more diverse than the typical UK undergraduate cohort, and its assessment structure is unusually modular (Chapter 2 §2.3). The generalisability of the present findings to other institutions, other modes of study, and other national contexts is therefore an open empirical question. The literature reviewed in Chapter 2 §2.5 includes studies on other datasets (e.g., the MOOC datasets used by Yang et al., 2015, and by Whitehill et al., 2015) that reach broadly similar conclusions about the predictive power of engagement features, but no cross-institutional comparison is performed in the present study. A future study that applied the same methodology to two or three comparable datasets would be the cleanest way to test the generalisability of the present findings.

### Limitation 2 — Withdrawn Students Excluded from the Modelling Matrix

The present study excluded Withdrawn students from the modelling matrix (Chapter 3 §3.4.2). This is a deliberate methodological choice: the binary target requires a known outcome, and Withdrawn students have no final outcome that can be unambiguously classified as Pass or Fail. The cost of this choice is that the resulting model cannot be used to predict withdrawal, and a deployment institution that wanted to flag students at risk of either failing or withdrawing would need a separate model. There is a small literature on withdrawal prediction using OULAD (e.g., the work reviewed in Chapter 2 §2.5), but it is not addressed in this study. The exclusion also means that the 31.4 per cent Fail prevalence reported in §4.5 is computed on a non-representative subset of the original OULAD population; the prevalence among all students (including Withdrawn) is closer to 21 per cent.

### Limitation 3 — Static Features, No Temporal Trajectory

All twenty-one features in the present study are aggregated over the full presentation (Chapter 3 §3.5). The model therefore predicts the final outcome from the full feature set, not from features observed up to a particular week of the presentation. This is a stronger prediction problem than the one an institution would face in practice: an institution typically wants to flag a student *during* the presentation, while there is still time to intervene. The methodology can be extended to temporal prediction by aggregating the features up to each week of the presentation and training a sequence of weekly models, but that extension is not implemented in the present study. A future study that trained weekly models would be able to quantify how early in the presentation the model can identify failing students, and that information would be directly relevant to institutional deployment.

### Limitation 4 — Threshold Sensitivity Not Analysed

Both models were tuned on F1 with a default 0.5 probability threshold, and the F1 gap of 0.020 between the Random Forest and the Logistic Regression depends on that threshold (Chapter 4 §4.5). A threshold-sensitivity analysis in which the operating point was varied between 0.3 and 0.7 would clarify whether the gap is stable across thresholds or whether it is an artefact of the default. The present study did not perform this analysis. The omission is consequential: the institutional deployment decision depends on the operating point, and an institution that wanted to catch as many failing students as possible would set a lower threshold than one that wanted to minimise false positives. Without the threshold-sensitivity analysis, the present study cannot recommend a specific operating point.

### Limitation 5 — Cross-Method Comparison on Different Feature Scales

The cross-model comparison in §4.8 is partly confounded by the fact that the two classifiers see the features on different scales: the Logistic Regression pipeline standardises the features before fitting, while the Random Forest fits on the original-scale features (Chapter 3 §3.7). The SHAP values are computed on those same scales, so the LinearSHAP values are in log-odds units on the standardised scale and the TreeSHAP values are in probability units on the original scale. A robustness check in which the Random Forest was also evaluated on the standardised features would clarify whether the divergence reported in §4.8 is driven by model family or by feature scale. The present study did not perform this check, and the author cannot, on the basis of the present results alone, separate the two effects. This limitation is flagged explicitly so that the conclusions in §5.2 are not over-interpreted.
