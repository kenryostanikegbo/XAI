## Summary

This study set out to investigate whether explainable machine-learning models could be used to predict student failure on the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD), and whether the explanations those models produce would be operationally useful to institutional stakeholders. The investigation was structured around six research objectives and four research questions, set in Chapter 1 §1.5, and pursued through a nine-phase implementation pipeline documented in Chapter 3. Chapter 4 reported the analytical findings. This final chapter turns those findings into conclusions and recommendations.

### The Problem and the Dataset

Student retention is a long-standing concern in higher education, and the growth of virtual learning environments has made it possible to collect the engagement data needed to study the problem at scale (Chapter 2 §2.2). The Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD) is the largest publicly available dataset of this kind: 32,593 students, seven relational tables, and approximately ten million virtual-learning-environment interactions (Kuzilek et al., 2017). The dataset is licensed under CC-BY 4.0, which makes it suitable for reproducible academic research. The present study used six of the seven OULAD tables — `studentInfo`, `studentVle`, `vle`, `assessments`, `studentAssessment`, and `courses` — to construct a binary classification problem: predict whether a student who completed a presentation would Pass or Fail, excluding Withdrawn students from the analysis.

### The Pipeline

Chapter 3 documented the methodological choices. Twenty-one features were engineered in four groups (six demographic, seven virtual-learning-environment engagement, five assessment, three course-context), and the binary target was constructed by mapping Pass and Distinction to 0 and Fail to 1 (§3.5). The modelling matrix contained 44 columns after one-hot encoding. A stratified 70/30 train/test split with `random_state=42` produced a training set on which Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE; Chawla et al., 2002) was applied within each cross-validation fold to address the 31.4 per cent Fail prevalence (§3.6). Two classifiers were trained: Logistic Regression with L2 regularisation and the lbfgs solver, and Random Forest with 500 trees and a maximum depth of 15, both tuned on F1 by five-fold stratified grid search (§3.7). SHAP values were computed at the global and local levels using LinearSHAP for the Logistic Regression and TreeSHAP for the Random Forest (§3.9). A 300-row stratified subsample of the test set was used to make TreeSHAP tractable, and that subsample was reused in §4.7 for the local explanations.

### The Headline Findings

Four headline findings emerged from the analysis in Chapter 4. First, both models achieve strong predictive performance: ROC-AUC of 0.957 (Logistic Regression) and 0.962 (Random Forest) on the held-out test set, with the Random Forest retaining a small lead on five of six metrics and the Logistic Regression retaining a marginal lead on precision alone (§4.5). Second, the two models identify different drivers of student failure: the Logistic Regression top-10 is dominated by demographic and course-context features, while the Random Forest top-10 is dominated by engagement and assessment features; only four features appear in both top-10 lists (§4.6). Third, the cross-model rank correlation of the 44-feature importance vectors is 0.10 (p = 0.508), and the top-10 Jaccard index is 0.25, confirming that the two models identify substantially different factors (§4.8). Fourth, at the local level, the two models agree on the four corners of the confusion matrix but disagree on the two borderline cases near the decision boundary, with the Logistic Regression predicting Fail and the Random Forest predicting Pass for one borderline student (§4.7).

### What Was Not Found

Two negative findings are worth flagging at the summary stage. The two classifiers are not statistically distinguishable on ROC-AUC at conventional thresholds given the gap of 0.005, and the F1 gap of 0.020 depends on the choice of probability threshold (§4.5). The threshold-sensitivity analysis needed to claim a stable performance gap was not performed and is left for future work. The cross-model Spearman correlation does not reach statistical significance at conventional thresholds, but the qualitative pattern (demographic-led Logistic Regression versus engagement-led Random Forest) is consistent across all four analytical chapters and is not in doubt (§4.8). Both negative findings are reported in the spirit of Chapter 2 §2.4: an absence of evidence is itself evidence, and a study that reports only its positive findings is harder to trust than one that reports both.
