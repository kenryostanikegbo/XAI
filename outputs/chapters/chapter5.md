# Chapter 5; Discussion, Conclusions, and Recommendations

## 5.1 Summary

This study set out to investigate whether explainable machine-learning models could be used to predict student failure on the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD), and whether the explanations those models produce would be operationally useful to institutional stakeholders. The investigation was structured around six research objectives and four research questions (Chapter 1 Section 1.5), and pursued through a nine-phase implementation pipeline documented in Chapter 3. Chapter 4 reported the analytical findings; this final chapter turns those findings into conclusions and recommendations.

The Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD; Kuzilek et al., 2017) is the largest publicly available dataset of this kind: 32,593 students, seven relational tables, and approximately ten million virtual-learning-environment interactions, licensed under CC-BY 4.0. The present study used six of the seven tables; `studentInfo`, `studentVle`, `vle`, `assessments`, `studentAssessment`, and `courses`; to construct a binary classification problem: predict whether a student who completed a presentation would Pass or Fail, excluding Withdrawn students from the analysis.

Four headline findings emerged from Chapter 4. First, both models achieve strong predictive performance: ROC-AUC of 0.957 (Logistic Regression) and 0.962 (Random Forest), with the Random Forest retaining a small lead on five of six metrics and the Logistic Regression retaining a marginal lead on precision alone. Second, the two models identify different drivers of student failure: the Logistic Regression top-10 is dominated by demographic and course-context features, while the Random Forest top-10 is dominated by engagement and assessment features, with only four features appearing in both lists. Third, the cross-model rank correlation of the 44-feature importance vectors is 0.10 (p = 0.508), and the top-10 Jaccard index is 0.25. Fourth, at the local level, the two models agree on direction for four of six illustrative cases but disagree on the two borderline cases near the decision boundary.

Two negative findings are also worth flagging: the two classifiers are not statistically distinguishable on ROC-AUC given the gap of 0.005, and the F1 gap of 0.020 depends on the choice of probability threshold. Both negative findings are reported in the spirit of Chapter 2 Section 2.4: an absence of evidence is itself evidence.

## 5.2 Conclusions Against the Research Objectives

The six research objectives set in Chapter 1 Section 1.5.1–Section 1.5.6 were met. Objective 1 (integrate OULAD into a modelling-ready matrix) was met in Chapter 3 Section 3.4–Section 3.5, producing an integrated matrix of 22,311 student-presentation rows and 44 columns after one-hot encoding, reproducible from a clean clone in under 45 minutes. Objective 2 (train and tune the two classifiers) was met in Chapter 3 Section 3.7, with both classifiers tuned by five-fold stratified grid search on F1 inside an `imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline` to prevent leakage. Objective 3 (compare on a six-metric framework) was met in Chapter 4 Section 4.5. Objective 4 (global SHAP explanations) was met in Chapter 4 Section 4.6, with the Logistic Regression top-10 dominated by demographic and course-context features and the Random Forest top-10 dominated by engagement and assessment features. Objective 5 (local SHAP explanations on six illustrative cases) was met in Chapter 4 Section 4.7, with the borderline cases exposing the cross-model disagreement most clearly. Objective 6 (cross-model comparison) was met in Chapter 4 Section 4.8, quantifying the divergence as Spearman ρ = 0.10 and top-10 Jaccard = 0.25.

## 5.3 Limitations of the Study

Five limitations bound the present findings, and each is worth stating explicitly.

**Limitation 1; Single dataset, single institution.** The Open University is one institution, and the OULAD is one dataset. The Open University is unusual in three respects: it is a distance-learning institution, its student body is older and more diverse than the typical UK undergraduate cohort, and its assessment structure is unusually modular (Chapter 2 Section 2.3). The generalisability of the present findings to other institutions, other modes of study, and other national contexts is therefore an open empirical question. The literature reviewed in Chapter 2 Section 2.5 includes studies on other datasets (for example, MOOC datasets) that reach broadly similar conclusions about the predictive power of engagement features, but no cross-institutional comparison is performed in the present study. A future study that applied the same methodology to two or three comparable datasets would be the cleanest way to test the generalisability of the present findings.

**Limitation 2; Withdrawn students excluded from the modelling matrix.** The exclusion of Withdrawn students is a deliberate methodological choice: the binary target requires a known outcome, and Withdrawn students have no final outcome that can be unambiguously classified as Pass or Fail. The cost is that the resulting model cannot be used to predict withdrawal, and a deployment institution that wanted to flag students at risk of either failing or withdrawing would need a separate model. There is a small literature on withdrawal prediction using OULAD, but it is not addressed in this study. The exclusion also means that the 31.4 per cent Fail prevalence reported in Section 4.5 is computed on a non-representative subset of the original OULAD population; the prevalence among all students (including Withdrawn) is closer to 21 per cent.

**Limitation 3; Static features, no temporal trajectory.** All twenty-one features in the present study are aggregated over the full presentation. The model therefore predicts the final outcome from the full feature set, not from features observed up to a particular week of the presentation. This is a stronger prediction problem than the one an institution would face in practice: an institution typically wants to flag a student *during* the presentation, while there is still time to intervene. The methodology can be extended to temporal prediction by aggregating the features up to each week of the presentation and training a sequence of weekly models, but that extension is not implemented in the present study.

**Limitation 4; Threshold sensitivity not analysed.** Both models were tuned on F1 with a default 0.5 probability threshold, and the F1 gap of 0.020 between the Random Forest and the Logistic Regression depends on that threshold. A threshold-sensitivity analysis in which the operating point was varied between 0.3 and 0.7 would clarify whether the gap is stable across thresholds or whether it is an artefact of the default. The present study did not perform this analysis, and the omission is consequential because the institutional deployment decision depends on the operating point. An institution that wanted to catch as many failing students as possible would set a lower threshold than one that wanted to minimise false positives.

**Limitation 5; Cross-method comparison on different feature scales.** The cross-model comparison in Section 4.8 is partly confounded by the fact that the two classifiers see the features on different scales: the Logistic Regression pipeline standardises the features before fitting, while the Random Forest fits on the original-scale features. The SHAP values are computed on those same scales, so the LinearSHAP values are in log-odds units on the standardised scale and the TreeSHAP values are in probability units on the original scale. A robustness check in which the Random Forest was also evaluated on the standardised features would clarify whether the divergence reported in Section 4.8 is driven by model family or by feature scale. The present study did not perform this check, and the author cannot, on the basis of the present results alone, separate the two effects.

All five limitations should be flagged when the present findings are read. They do not invalidate the qualitative conclusions, but they bound the conditions under which those conclusions hold.

## 5.4 Recommendations for Institutional Practice

Five recommendations follow from the present findings for any institution considering deployment of an explainable machine-learning system to predict student failure.

**Recommendation 1; Present both explanations side by side.** The two classifiers identify different drivers of student failure, and an institution that deploys only one will systematically bias the tutor's view of the student. The cleanest deployment is therefore a two-model deployment: both classifiers run, both predictions are presented, and both SHAP explanations are shown side by side.

**Recommendation 2; Use the Random Forest for catchment and the Logistic Regression for justification.** The Random Forest has higher recall (0.831 versus 0.789, Section 4.5) and so catches more failing students; the Logistic Regression explanation is easier to communicate to a non-technical audience because every feature contributes a single signed weight and there are no interactions to explain (Section 4.6). A natural hybrid deployment uses the Random Forest to flag students and the Logistic Regression to justify the flag.

**Recommendation 3; Treat the explanations as decision support, not decision automation.** SHAP values are statistical associations, not causal claims, and the institutional response to a flagged student should be informed by the explanation but not determined by it (Chapter 2 Section 2.4). A tutor who has never seen a SHAP plot cannot interpret one without training; an institution that deploys a SHAP dashboard without training invests in a liability rather than an asset.

**Recommendation 4; Validate on a subsequent cohort before scaling up.** The cross-validated performance estimates reported in Section 4.5 are honest within-cohort estimates, not cross-cohort estimates. An institution that intends to deploy on a subsequent cohort should validate the model on that cohort before scaling up, including a spot-check of the explanations on a handful of students.

**Recommendation 5; Invest in tutor training on SHAP outputs.** The training should cover what a SHAP value means (a feature contribution to a single prediction, not a causal effect), how to read the waterfall plot, and how to act on borderline cases where the SHAP sum is small and the prediction is unstable.

## 5.5 Recommendations for Future Research

Six directions for future research follow from the limitations identified in Section 5.3 and from the open questions raised in Section 4.5–Section 4.8.

**Direction 1; Temporal prediction at multiple points within the presentation.** The present study predicts the final outcome from features aggregated over the full presentation. A natural extension would predict the final outcome from features aggregated up to each week of the presentation, producing a sequence of weekly models. This would address how early in the presentation the model can identify failing students, whether the feature ranking changes across weeks, and whether borderline cases shift across weeks.

**Direction 2; Threshold-sensitivity analysis for operating-point selection.** A threshold-sensitivity analysis in which the operating point was varied between 0.3 and 0.7 would clarify whether the F1 gap of 0.020 is stable across operating points or an artefact of the default. The present study did not perform this analysis because the deployment context is institution-specific.

**Direction 3; Cross-method robustness check on standardised features.** A second Random Forest trained on the standardised features would clarify whether the divergence reported in Section 4.8 is driven by model family or by feature scale. The cost of the robustness check is modest because the methodology is already implemented.

**Direction 4; Cross-institutional comparison.** Applying the same methodology to two or three comparable datasets (for example, a US MOOC dataset or a European university learning-records-store dump) would test the generalisability of the engagement-led ranking. Either replication or non-replication would be informative.

**Direction 5; Causal interpretation of feature contributions.** A future study that combined the predictive model with a causal-inference framework (for example, a structural causal model or a double-machine-learning estimator) would identify which feature contributions represent causal levers and which represent mere correlations. The institutional payoff is large but the methodological cost is high, because the OULAD dataset does not contain a randomised intervention by design.

**Direction 6; Beyond binary classification.** A three-class problem (Pass, Fail, Withdrawn) and a regression problem predicting the final mark on a continuous scale would extend the methodology to more nuanced views of student risk.

## 5.6 Concluding Remarks

This study set out to investigate whether explainable machine learning could predict student failure on the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset, and whether the explanations the models produced would be operationally useful. The investigation answered four research questions, met six research objectives, and produced a result that is both reassuring and uncomfortable. The reassuring part is that the methodology works: both classifiers achieve strong predictive performance, the SHAP-based explanations are stable enough to inspect at the per-instance level, and the pipeline is reproducible from a clean clone in under 45 minutes. The uncomfortable part is that the two classifiers do not agree on the reasons: the Spearman rank correlation between the two mean-|SHAP| vectors is 0.10, the top-10 Jaccard index is 0.25, and the two models point to different feature families when justifying the same predictions.

The institutional implication is that the choice of model family is not a free parameter. An institution that deploys only one of the two classifiers will see only one of the two explanations, and will therefore systematically bias its view of its students. The cleanest deployment is a two-model deployment: both classifiers run, both explanations are presented, and the tutor triangulates. This is operationally modest; it adds one model to the pipeline; but it removes a known source of systematic bias. The borderline cases are exactly the cases most in need of human judgement, and the institutional response should be most intensive for those cases.

The methodological implication is that the present study cannot, on the basis of observational data alone, separate model-family effects from scale effects or feature-engineering effects. A future study that trained both classifiers on the same standardised features would clarify this, but the present study does not, and the conclusions are bounded accordingly.

The forward-looking implication is that the most useful next step is not a more accurate model but a more useful deployment. The present study has shown that the methodology can predict student failure at a level of accuracy that would be operationally useful, and that the explanations can be inspected at the per-instance level. What remains is to deploy the methodology in an institutional setting, validate it on a subsequent cohort, and refine it on the basis of tutor feedback. Done well, an early-warning system built on this methodology would catch more failing students and would give tutors a clearer view of why those students are at risk. Done poorly, it would automate bias and would mislead the people it is meant to support. The choice between those outcomes is not a methodological choice; it is an institutional one.
